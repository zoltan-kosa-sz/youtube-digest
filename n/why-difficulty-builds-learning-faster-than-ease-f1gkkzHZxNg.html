A life

Why difficulty builds learning faster than ease

Psych Tunes / 2026-09-20 / Watch

Use

  1. Swap review for retrieval practice — close the book and recall everything from memory, not by re-reading. The struggle to retrieve is where the learning happens.
  2. Space out study sessions until material fades — wait until recall feels hard before reviewing again. Repetition in one sitting builds nothing; the gap between sessions is the active ingredient.
  3. Interleave topics instead of blocking them — mix problems from different chapters/methods instead of doing 10 of one type, then 10 of the next. It produces worse performance during practice but far better transfer.
  4. Explain concepts from memory with no notes — when you hit the wall (a sentence that dissolves, a technical term you can't rephrase), that's the exact coordinate of what you don't know; go fill it.
  5. Connect new facts to existing knowledge, not hoarding isolated facts — every connection turns two things into one cheaper thing to store. After a year of building these patterns, new material stops being a stranger.

Bottom line

Learning speed depends on study method, not talent or effort volume. The fastest learners use "desirable difficulties"—conditions that feel slower and harder during learning but produce far superior long-term retention and transfer. The feeling of ease has been your entire life pointing you the wrong way.

Key ideas

  • Storage strength and retrieval strength move independently. Only retrieval strength shows up as current performance (feeling like you know something); only storage strength is actual learning. Information can feel solid today and vanish in nine days.
  • Difficulty is the mechanism of learning, not the price of it. Conditions that slow you down during learning often produce better long-term retention. When you successfully retrieve something that was faded and hard to find, the gain in storage strength is largest.0:00
  • The fluency illusion: ease is a false signal. Feeling like you know something because it's easy to process right now means nothing about whether you'll remember it without the page, under pressure, or on Thursday. Rereading produces smooth fluency and zero learning; testing produces struggle and permanent memory.4:10
  • Retrieval practice beats rereading by an enormous margin. After students learned Swahili-English pairs and kept re-testing them, they recalled 80% a week later; those who kept re-studying recalled about one-third. Even worse: the re-study group was more confident they'd remember.6:46
  • Interleaving forces you to identify which method applies. When problems are blocked (10 of type A, then 10 of type B), performance during practice is 90% accurate—but transfer is poor. Interleaved practice drops performance to 60% accuracy during practice but nearly triples scores on the final test.9:23
  • Chunks, not facts, are the unfair speed. Chess masters reconstructing a board from a few seconds' viewing vastly outperform beginners—but only with real positions, not random ones. They hold five familiar patterns; beginners hold seven isolated pieces. Expert-level speed comes from thousands of stored patterns, not faster processing or better memory.11:29
  • Learning is an output, not a starting condition. After a year of building network connections through retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, and generation, new material arrives as a variation on something you already have. People will call it a gift or natural talent. It's trails that were widened quietly, one gap at a time.13:01

Reality check

  • SupportedRobert Bjork (UCLA) and the storage-strength/retrieval-strength model — Bjork's research on desirable difficulties and the 1992 theory of disuse are foundational in cognitive psychology and widely confirmed.
  • Supported2006 Roediger and Karpicke study — showed restudy outperforms testing only at 5 minutes; testing vastly outperforms at 1 week.
  • SupportedLearning styles assessment has no evidence base — Pashler et al. (2008) reviewed the entire literature and found almost no studies with adequate methodology, and those that did often contradicted the learning-styles hypothesis.
  • Supported2008 Karpicke and Roediger Science study on Swahili-English pairs — confirmed 80% retention with continued testing vs. ~33% with continued study after one week.
  • SupportedRohrer's 7th-grade interleaving study — 72% for interleaved practice vs. 38% for blocked practice, with interleaving producing lower performance during practice.
  • SupportedChase and Simon 1973 chess study — masters outperformed beginners with real positions but not random ones, confirming chunking hypothesis.
  • SupportedHerbert Simon Nobel Prize and AI — Simon won the 1978 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences and co-created the Logic Theorist (1956), one of the first AI programs.
