Extracting client budgets with price anchoring

2026-09-20 / Watch

Use

  1. State your price range first — in your opening conversation, before the client mentions budget. This anchors the negotiation in your direction and prevents them from anchoring lower.
  2. Use ranges to extract the real budget — when a client says they don't know their budget, present brackets (100k–500k, 500k–1M, over 1M) and ask which bracket fits. They'll pick one, then you narrow further. People have budgets but hide them until pricing feels concrete.
  3. Disqualify early — if a client can't afford you or has multiple decision-makers, walk away rather than negotiate down. Budget mismatch and group approval delays are both expensive for you.

Bottom line

Speak the price first. The anchoring effect means whoever says the first number shapes the entire negotiation, and money is a disqualifier—learn it early so you don't waste time on wrong-fit clients.

Key ideas

  • Price anchoring works because the first number spoken anchors the entire negotiation. If you don't say it first, the anchor works against you.0:31
  • Money is a disqualifier, not a tactic. If someone can't afford you or isn't a good fit, knowing early saves both of you time.3:06
  • Give price ranges, not vague answers. People always have a budget but withhold it until pricing feels real; ranges make it safe to tell the truth.5:41
  • Disqualify clients with multiple decision-makers. If six people must approve, complexity multiplies; either work with all of them together (charge 10× more) or walk away.5:11
  • Reframe yourself as their advocate, not a seller. "I represent you, not the house. Why would you lie to me?"—this builds enough trust that they reveal their actual constraints.7:46
  • Have this conversation voice-to-voice, never email. Email and DM let clients dodge; phone or in-person lets them "hear your smile" and sense sincerity.6:12
