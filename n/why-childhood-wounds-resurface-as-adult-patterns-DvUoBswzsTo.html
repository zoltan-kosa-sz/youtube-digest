A life

Why childhood wounds resurface as adult patterns

Doug Bopst / 2026-09-11 / Watch

Key ideas

  • Beliefs vs. present reality — "the world is against me" is a childhood-formed belief, not a description of your current situation; healing happens by addressing it in the present, not by re-litigating the past.
  • Most clinicians aren't trauma-trained — Maté says average physicians, psychiatrists, and CBT/DBT-trained therapists change behavior/beliefs but rarely address the underlying wound; look for trauma-informed modalities (somatic experiencing, sensorimotor psychotherapy, EMDR, IFS, compassionate inquiry).
  • Triggers are small; the charge is yours — an external trigger only detonates pre-existing "ammunition" from childhood; the useful question is curious ("I wonder why this came up") not accusatory ("why is this happening to me").
  • The self-critical inner voice is a child's old protective strategy — believing "something's wrong with me, I can fix it" was safer for a child than believing "the world is dangerous and I'm unlovable." Treat the voice as a young, well-meaning "stupid friend," not an enemy.
  • Parenting mismatch — humans evolved raising kids in tribes with constant adult contact; isolated nuclear-family + screen-heavy parenting is a poor fit. Parents' own emotional regulation, unconditional acceptance, letting kids feel (not suppress) emotions, and screen-free outdoor play matter most, especially in the first three years.

Use

  • When self-critical, swap "why am I like this" for "I wonder why this came up" — same event, less shame, more information.
  • Before blaming someone for "triggering" you, ask what stored reaction you're supplying — the trigger is small, the charge is yours.
  • Name the inner critical voice, guess its age, and address it directly ("thanks, I've got this now") instead of arguing with it.
  • If seeking therapy for recurring patterns (not just symptom management), specifically ask a prospective therapist whether they work with trauma, not just CBT/behavior change.
  • Around young kids: default to no screens, more unstructured outdoor play, and manage your own stress — they absorb your emotional state.

Reality check

  • SupportedNamed therapies and creators (somatic experiencing/Peter Levine, sensorimotor psychotherapy/Pat Ogden, IFS/Richard Schwartz) — correctly attributed, real established modalities.
  • Caveat"Screens... shown on brain scans" to interfere with healthy brain development — real studies (MRI/longitudinal) do link early heavy screen use to altered brain structure and function, but the research is largely correlational, effect depends on content/context (e.g. parent reading offsets it), not the clean causal "screens damage brains" Maté implies.
  • CaveatPsychiatrists get "nothing" trauma training re: depression/anxiety/ADHD — directionally true (trauma-informed training is patchy and inconsistently taught in residencies) but "nothing" overstates it; some programs do cover it.
  • WrongADHD has a "traumatic basis" — mentioned in passing; mainstream science puts ADHD heritability at 70–80% (twin/family studies) and views trauma as an exacerbating factor, not the underlying cause as Maté's broader body of work claims. This is a long-standing, well-documented dispute between Maté and ADHD researchers, not settled science.
  • WrongThich Nhat Hanh "died about a year ago" — he died January 2022; this video was published January 2024, so it was roughly two years, not one — minor but a factual slip stated as fact.
