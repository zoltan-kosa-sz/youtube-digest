What stays valuable when AI commoditizes software

The Diary Of A CEO Clips / 2026-09-11 / Watch

Use

  1. If building a product: ask what a small (500–1,000 customer) niche SaaS would look like in your own domain, built with AI at a fraction of past cost — then bundle it with community/training/events rather than selling the software alone (the bundle, not the tool, is the moat).
  2. Audit your own content for "informational" vs. "irreplaceably human" — swap generic takes/quotes for stories only you can tell; that's what survives commoditization.
  3. Stop optimizing for one big bet — build a small portfolio of income streams (product, content, coaching, speaking) instead of one product/service.

Bottom line

AI collapses the cost of building software and content to near zero, which means both become commodities fast — the only durable value left is what's irreplaceably human: personal story, real-world presence, and relationships with an audience. Small, bundled, lifestyle-scale businesses are now easier to build than ever; big ones are harder, because disruption cycles now run in months, not years.

Key ideas

  • AI turned SaaS from an elite game into a cheap one. Building software used to require 10–30 developers, millions in funding, and 10,000 customers to break even; now niche tools with 500–1,000 customers are viable — but the software itself is easy to copy, so it needs community, training, or events attached to stay defensible.
  • Ease and value move in opposite directions. The moment something (content, software) becomes cheap and easy to produce, everyone does it, and it stops being valuable — so "just start a SaaS" or "just make content" advice ages fast.
  • Ask "can a kid in Mumbai (or an AI) do this?" The filter for what's still worth building: physical presence, live events, and things that require having actually lived an experience don't commoditize.
  • "Relatable beats impressive." Personal, specific stories (a struggle, a proposal, a lived experience) build audience connection and outperform generic "impressive" or inspirational content — informational content ("this is how X works") is becoming a commodity.
  • Blue oceans now close in months, not years. Marketing/social agencies were a blue ocean around 2014; by 2024 most of Priestley's peers running agencies were struggling against compressed margins — AI accelerates how fast an open market gets crowded.
  • Most entrepreneurs don't want "big." They want a flexible, ~$1–5M revenue, ~10-person lifestyle business — which he argues is now easier to build than ever, while scaling past that is getting harder because bigger businesses absorb more disruption.
  • Income should be an ecosystem, not one product. People making the most money now stack many small revenue lines (speaking, ads, sponsorships, royalties, equity, coaching) rather than relying on a single offer.

Reality check

  • SupportedSpotify's best developers "haven't written a line of code since December" thanks to AI. Confirmed: Spotify co-CEO Gustav Söderström said this on the Feb 2026 Q4 earnings call; developers now supervise AI-generated code rather than write it.
  • Mixed"$280 billion wiped off legal/data-tech companies in the last week, ~20% down in 2026." Directionally real — reporting shows roughly $300B lost across legal/software-data indexes in 2026 (Thomson Reuters −16%, LegalZoom ~−20%, RELX −14%, Wolters Kluwer −13%) — but the specific $280B/one-week framing isn't traceable to a single confirmed event; treat the number as approximate, the trend as real.
  • Mixedthe Harlow "wire mother" monkey grows up "to be a psychopath." The 1950s-60s Harlow experiments are real and did show monkeys raised on a wire-only surrogate developed severe social/emotional dysfunction (real finding: contact comfort matters more than feeding for attachment) — but "psychopath" is a dramatized label, not the term used in the actual research or its documented outcomes.
  • Disputeduniversity loans "distorted" career choice and caused the plumber/electrician shortage. Real shortage, but multi-causal per labor-market reporting: an aging trades workforce nearing retirement, cuts to vocational education, and long apprenticeship timelines are cited alongside student debt — presenting loans as *the* cause oversimplifies it.
